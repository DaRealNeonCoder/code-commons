An embedding is a learned vector representation. Unlike the hand-chosen features in the Intro course, an embedding's coordinates do not have a fixed human meaning. The useful property is relational: items with similar roles should land near one another under a chosen similarity measure.

## Similarity is a training signal

Given an anchor `a`, a positive example `p`, and negatives `n₁...nₖ`, a simple contrastive objective tries to make:

```text
similarity(a, p) high
similarity(a, nᵢ) low
```

One softmax-style loss is:

```text
loss = -log( exp(sim(a,p) / temperature) / sum_j exp(sim(a,j) / temperature) )
```

Temperature controls how sharply the loss focuses on the hardest alternatives. Lower temperature makes small similarity differences matter more, but can make optimization brittle.

## Where positives come from

For images, two augmented views of one image can be a positive pair. For text, nearby context, a query and its relevant passage, or two descriptions of one item can be positives. The model learns from the relationship, not from a manually assigned class for every possible concept.

## Geometry, not labels alone

Normalize embeddings when cosine similarity is the intended geometry. Watch for shortcuts: a model may encode camera style, document length, or another accidental cue instead of the semantic relationship. Evaluate retrieval with held-out relationships and inspect nearest neighbors, not only the training loss.

> [!TIP]
> An embedding is an interface. Decide what “nearby” should mean, then choose the data pairs, similarity function, and evaluation examples to match that meaning.
