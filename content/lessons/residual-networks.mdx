Adding layers can increase expressive power, but a very deep plain network can be difficult to optimize. Gradients may shrink as they travel backward, and a new layer may damage a representation that was already useful.

## Learn a residual, not a replacement

A residual block computes:

```text
output = activation(x + F(x))
```

`F(x)` is the learned change. If the best behavior is close to the identity function, the block can make `F(x)` close to zero and pass `x` through the skip path. The skip connection gives both activations and gradients a shorter route.

If input and output shapes differ, a learned projection can transform the skip path:

```text
output = activation(projection(x) + F(x))
```

This is an architectural pattern, not a special loss function. Batch normalization, activation placement, and bottleneck blocks are important engineering variations, but the identity-plus-change idea is the foundation.

## When residuals help

Residual connections do not guarantee a better model. They make optimization easier by giving a block a safe identity-like starting behavior. You still need a validation protocol, suitable normalization, enough data, and a sensible learning rate. A deeper model with skip connections can then improve representations without requiring every layer to relearn the entire input.

> [!TIP]
> “Deeper” and “harder to optimize” are not synonyms. Ask whether the architecture gives gradients a direct path and whether a block can represent “do nothing.”
