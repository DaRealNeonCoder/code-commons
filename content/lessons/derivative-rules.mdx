The previous lesson estimated a slope by looking at nearby points. That is useful for checking our work, but training code also needs quick, exact rules. These rules describe the same local rate of change.

## Three rules for now

| Function | Derivative | What it says |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `xⁿ` | `n × xⁿ⁻¹` | powers get one degree lower |
| `sin(x)` | `cos(x)` | the slope cycles with the wave |
| `exp(x)` | `exp(x)` | the exponential is its own slope |

The derivative of a constant is `0`. Derivatives add, so the derivative of `x² + 3x + 4` is `2x + 3`. When a function has one function inside another, use the **chain rule**; that is the next lesson.

At `x = 3`, the derivative of `x² + 3x + 4` is `2 × 3 + 3 = 9`. A numerical estimate with a small step should be close to `9`, giving us a useful test of the algebra.

## Why exponentials appear

The exponential `exp(x)` grows quickly and is always positive. Its shifted version

```text
sigmoid(x) = 1 / (1 + exp(-x))
```

turns any real number into a value between `0` and `1`, which makes it useful for a probability-like prediction. We will meet it again in logistic regression and neural networks.

## A derivative is not an average slope

The slope between two far-apart points is an **average** change. A derivative is the slope at one point, found by shrinking the gap. In code, choose `h` small enough to be useful but not so small that floating-point rounding overwhelms the subtraction.

> [!TIP]
> Learn the rule, then check it numerically. The two methods are different paths to the same local slope and are excellent at catching a missing factor or sign.
